Exterior Painting Guide: Preparation, Paint Types, Weather & Professional Techniques

Exterior paint does more than improve the appearance of a property. The right coating, applied to a properly prepared surface, helps protect walls and woodwork from everyday weather exposure. This guide explains how to plan an exterior painting project in the UK.

Published: 7 November 2026
Published by: EveroDecor – Professional Painting & Decorating, London
Editor & Adaptation: Daniel Pedro V Sousa

Exterior painting is different from decorating indoors. Outside, paint is exposed to rain, sunlight, temperature changes, wind, pollution and biological growth. A coating that looks good on the day it is applied may fail prematurely if the wall is damp, the old paint is unstable or the weather is unsuitable.

Before choosing a colour, identify the material you are painting. Render, brick, pebbledash, timber and previously coated masonry each have different characteristics. The right preparation and paint system should be selected for the actual substrate, not simply because a product is labelled as exterior paint.

The short answer: inspect the property, identify and address the cause of any damp or coating failure, clean and repair the surface, and use a compatible primer or stabilising treatment where specified. Apply an exterior coating suitable for the substrate, following the product's temperature, weather, coverage and recoat instructions.

Which exterior surfaces can be painted?

Different parts of a property's exterior may need different products. A masonry paint suitable for render is not automatically suitable for timber windows, metal railings or plastic components.

Rendered walls

Render can provide a suitable base for exterior masonry coatings when it is sound, sufficiently cured and dry. Inspect for cracks, hollow or loose areas, powdering and signs of water ingress. New render may need a specified curing period before painting. Follow the coating manufacturer's guidance for the particular render type.

Brickwork

Brick is porous and can hold moisture, particularly where pointing is damaged or the wall is exposed to driving rain. Before painting, check the condition of the bricks and mortar joints. Painting may change how the wall dries, so the coating must be appropriate for the construction and moisture conditions. Resolve defects and persistent damp before applying paint.

Pebbledash and textured masonry

Textured surfaces have more peaks and recesses than smooth render. They can require more paint and careful application to achieve consistent coverage. Loose aggregate, failing paint and dirt should be dealt with first. Choose a coating intended for the relevant masonry surface and use application tools suited to its texture.

Exterior timber

Fascias, soffits, doors, frames and other timber elements need a suitable exterior wood coating. Check for rot, open joints, peeling paint and exposed end grain. Repair or replace unsound timber before painting. Bare timber normally needs a compatible primer or preservative system as specified by the coating manufacturer.

Metal railings and other metalwork

Metal requires a coating system designed for the metal type and exposure. Remove loose rust and failing paint using an appropriate method, prepare the surface and apply a suitable metal primer where required. Do not use masonry paint as a substitute for a metal coating.

Exterior surface preparation: the most important stage

Paint cannot reliably stabilise a failing wall or solve the source of damp. Preparation should begin with an inspection to understand why the existing coating or substrate has deteriorated.

  1. Inspect the building: look for cracks, loose render, damaged pointing, failing paint, blocked gutters, leaking downpipes and defective seals.
  2. Identify moisture problems: investigate persistent damp, leaks or water penetration. Allow the substrate to dry and resolve the underlying issue before painting.
  3. Protect the surroundings: cover paving, plants, windows, doors, outdoor fittings and neighbouring surfaces. Consider wind direction when protecting the area from spray or splashes.
  4. Clean the surface: remove dirt, dust, cobwebs and other contamination using a method suitable for the substrate. Avoid excessive water pressure that could damage render, mortar or timber.
  5. Address biological growth: treat algae, moss or fungal growth with a suitable product according to its instructions. Do not simply paint over active growth.
  6. Remove loose coatings: scrape or prepare flaking and poorly bonded paint using an appropriate safe method. Feather sound edges where necessary.
  7. Repair defects: use compatible exterior fillers, masonry repair products or mortar for the relevant defect. Allow repairs to cure or dry as instructed.
  8. Prime where required: use the specified primer, stabilising solution or preparation coat for the substrate and coating system.

Older exterior coatings may contain hazardous substances, and some preparation methods can create harmful dust. Assess the risks before disturbing unknown coatings. Use appropriate protective equipment and follow current UK health and safety guidance.

Choosing exterior paint: masonry, wood and metal coatings

Exterior coatings are formulated for different materials and conditions. Read the product label and technical data sheet to confirm that the paint is suitable for the surface, exposure and intended application method.

Masonry paint

Masonry paint is designed for exterior mineral surfaces such as suitable render and masonry. Products differ in their finish, water resistance, breathability, flexibility and resistance to weathering. Some are designed for smooth render, while others are suitable for textured or previously painted masonry. Choose according to the substrate and the building's condition.

Exterior wood paint

Exterior wood coatings are designed to protect timber exposed to changing weather conditions. Depending on the product, they may form an opaque painted finish or a translucent protective finish. Check whether the coating is suitable for the specific timber, whether a primer is required and how exposed end grain and joints should be treated.

Exterior metal paint

Metal coatings may require a dedicated primer to help adhesion and protect against corrosion. The correct preparation and primer can differ between ferrous metals, galvanised steel and other non-ferrous surfaces. Follow the coating system's instructions rather than applying a general-purpose paint directly to every metal surface.

Exterior paint is available in different finishes and formulations. The appearance is important, but the coating's suitability for the substrate and exposure should take priority.

Can you paint outside in any weather?

No. Weather conditions have a direct effect on application, drying and the performance of exterior paint. A dry-looking wall is not necessarily ready to paint, and a warm afternoon does not guarantee that conditions will remain suitable overnight.

Rain and damp surfaces

Do not paint a wet surface or apply a coating when rain is likely to reach it before it has become sufficiently rain-resistant. Damp substrates can affect adhesion and drying. Check the product's instructions for the required dry conditions and protection from rain.

Temperature

Every product has its own permitted application temperature range. Check both air and surface temperatures, not just the weather app reading. Cold conditions can slow drying, while excessive heat can cause paint to dry too quickly and make it harder to maintain a consistent finish.

Direct sunlight

Strong direct sunlight can heat the surface and shorten the working time of some coatings. Where possible, plan the work to avoid painting very hot surfaces. Work in manageable sections and follow the manufacturer's application guidance.

Wind and humidity

Wind can carry dust and debris onto wet paint and make application less controlled. High humidity can slow drying. Consider the forecast, the exposure of the wall and the product's stated limits before starting.

How to apply masonry paint to exterior walls

Once the surface is sound, clean, dry and correctly prepared, apply the selected coating using the tools and method recommended by the manufacturer. A roller may be suitable for broad areas, while a brush is useful for edges, corners and textured details.

  1. Check the surface again: make sure repairs and preparation treatments have dried or cured as required.
  2. Protect adjoining areas: mask or cover windows, doors, paths, fixtures and surfaces that should not receive paint.
  3. Stir the paint thoroughly: follow the product instructions and use the recommended dilution, if any. Do not add water unless the manufacturer permits it.
  4. Work around details: use a brush for edges, reveals and awkward areas before or as you work across the main wall.
  5. Apply evenly: use a consistent method and maintain a workable edge. Avoid leaving heavy deposits in corners or texture.
  6. Allow the first coat to dry: observe the stated recoat interval and check that weather conditions remain suitable.
  7. Apply further coats: follow the specified number of coats and coverage guidance to achieve an even finish.
  8. Inspect the result: check the wall in natural light for missed patches, uneven coverage or runs once the coating has dried sufficiently.

Painting exterior timber and woodwork

Exterior timber needs particular attention because it can expand and contract as moisture levels change. Paint may fail if it is applied over rotten wood, damp timber or unstable old coatings. Repair or replace damaged sections before decorating.

  1. Inspect timber for rot, splits, open joints and signs of water penetration.
  2. Remove loose paint and prepare sound coatings as specified by the new paint system.
  3. Repair or replace defective timber and allow repairs to dry or cure.
  4. Prime bare timber and exposed areas with a compatible exterior wood primer where required.
  5. Pay attention to end grain, joints and vulnerable edges according to the manufacturer's guidance.
  6. Apply the recommended top coats, allowing the specified drying time between applications.

How many coats of exterior paint are needed?

The number of coats depends on the surface, the existing colour, the new colour, the condition of the substrate and the product specification. A sound previously painted wall may need a different system from bare render or a heavily repaired surface.

Many projects use a suitable primer or preparation coat where required, followed by two finishing coats. However, this is a planning guide rather than a universal rule. Some products specify a different system, and porous or textured surfaces may affect coverage. Use the manufacturer's stated coverage rate and do not stretch the paint beyond its recommended spread rate to make it go further.

How long does exterior paint take to dry?

Exterior paint drying times vary according to the product and the conditions. Temperature, humidity, airflow, sunlight, surface porosity and the thickness of the applied coat can all affect drying. A coating that is touch-dry may not yet be resistant to rain or ready for another coat.

Check the product data sheet for touch-dry, recoat and rain-resistance information. Protect the work from rain and contamination for the required period. If conditions change unexpectedly, do not assume that the paint has dried simply because the surface looks matt or feels dry.

Planning an exterior painting project in London

In London, exterior painting needs to be planned around variable weather, shaded elevations, traffic dust and the condition of older buildings. North-facing walls and sheltered areas may remain damp for longer, while exposed elevations can experience driving rain and stronger wind.

Before work begins, consider access, safe working platforms, protection of neighbouring properties and the time needed for repairs and drying. Some buildings may also have restrictions relating to listed status, conservation areas, leasehold arrangements or shared access. Check the relevant requirements before changing the external appearance of a property.

Safety and access when painting outside

Exterior painting can involve ladders, scaffolding, working at height, chemical cleaners and dust-producing preparation. Plan access properly and use suitable equipment. Do not overreach from a ladder or use improvised platforms. Larger or higher areas may require a competent contractor and suitable scaffold or access equipment.

Protect people, pets, plants, vehicles and neighbouring property from dust, splashes and overspray. Follow the safety instructions for cleaning products, primers and paints, including ventilation and personal protective equipment requirements.

Exterior painting FAQs

What is the best paint for exterior walls?

Choose a masonry coating designed for the specific surface and exposure conditions. The right product for render may not be the right product for every brick or textured wall. Check substrate suitability, moisture characteristics and the manufacturer's preparation guidance.

Can you paint exterior walls in the rain?

No. Do not paint a wet surface or when rain is likely to affect the coating before it has become sufficiently rain-resistant. Check the forecast and the product instructions for the full drying period.

How warm does it need to be to paint outside?

There is no universal temperature for all exterior paints. Check the product's permitted air and surface temperature range, and consider humidity, wind, direct sunlight and overnight conditions.

Do exterior walls need primer before masonry paint?

Not always. New, bare, porous, chalky or repaired surfaces may need a stabilising solution, primer or specified preparation coat. The correct treatment depends on the substrate and paint system.

How many coats of exterior paint are needed?

Many systems use a preparation coat where required and two finishing coats, but the exact specification depends on the product and surface. Follow the manufacturer's technical data sheet.

Can you paint over flaking exterior paint?

No. Loose or failing coatings should be investigated and removed using an appropriate safe method. Repair the substrate and address any underlying moisture problem before applying a compatible coating system.

Conclusion: a lasting exterior finish starts with the surface

Successful exterior painting is built on sound preparation, suitable products and the right weather conditions. Inspect the property carefully, resolve defects, prepare each substrate correctly and follow the coating manufacturer's instructions. Taking time to do these steps helps create a more consistent finish and reduces the risk of premature paint failure.
