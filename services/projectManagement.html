Painting & Decorating Project Management: Planning, Costs, Trades & Delivery

A successful decorating project is about more than choosing colours and applying paint. Clear planning, realistic budgets, reliable scheduling and good communication help coordinate the work from the first site visit to the final handover.

Published: 7 November 2026
Published by: EveroDecor – Professional Painting & Decorating, London
Editor & Adaptation: Daniel Pedro V Sousa

Painting and decorating projects can involve several connected activities: surface preparation, repairs, plastering, flooring, carpentry, painting, electrical work and final cleaning. Even a relatively small refurbishment can become difficult to coordinate when different trades, deliveries and drying times overlap.

Project management provides a structured way to organise those activities. It helps the client understand what is included, gives trades a clear sequence to follow and makes it easier to identify problems before they affect the programme or budget.

The short answer: define the scope, survey the property, estimate labour and materials, agree responsibilities, schedule work in the correct order, monitor progress and costs, manage changes formally, and complete a documented handover. A clear plan does not remove every uncertainty, but it makes decisions and responsibilities easier to manage.

What is project management in painting and decorating?

Project management is the coordination of a job from initial planning through to completion. In a decorating or refurbishment project, this can include understanding the client's requirements, arranging surveys, preparing a work schedule, coordinating trades, ordering materials, checking progress and keeping the client informed.

The project manager may be responsible for the whole refurbishment or only for a defined part of the work. For example, a client might appoint one contractor to manage painting and decorating while a separate specialist is responsible for flooring, plumbing or electrical work. The boundaries should be clear before work starts.

Stage 1: Initial consultation and site survey

Before preparing a quotation or schedule, understand what the client wants to achieve and inspect the property. A site survey turns a general request into a more realistic description of the work.

Understand the client's requirements

Discuss the areas to be renovated, the expected finish, preferred colours and materials, practical constraints and the client's priorities. Confirm whether the property will be occupied during the work and whether particular rooms need to remain accessible.

Inspect the condition of the property

Look at walls, ceilings, woodwork, doors, floors and any areas affected by damp, cracks or previous repairs. Record defects that may need further investigation or specialist attention. Do not assume that every visible defect can be resolved within a standard painting allowance.

Measure and record the scope

Measure the relevant areas and record quantities where they affect labour or materials. Note ceiling heights, access, furniture, protection requirements, the number of doors and windows, and any unusual details. Photographs can help record the starting condition, with the client's permission.

Identify constraints and dependencies

Check access times, parking, building rules, lift availability, waste arrangements, delivery restrictions and any required approvals. Identify work that must happen before decorating can begin, such as leak repairs, plastering or electrical alterations.

Stage 2: Define the project scope

A clear scope of work explains what will be done, where it will be done and what standard or finish is expected. It also helps prevent misunderstandings about items that were discussed informally but never included in the quotation.

Example scope structure for a decorating project
Work areaExample description
WallsProtect, prepare, fill minor defects, sand and apply specified finish coats.
CeilingsPrepare the surface and apply the agreed ceiling paint system.
WoodworkPrepare and paint skirting boards, architraves and door frames.

Separate included work from exclusions and provisional items. For example, extensive plaster repairs, concealed damage, specialist electrical work or replacement of rotten timber may need a separate assessment and approval.

Stage 3: Estimate labour, materials and costs

A useful estimate should reflect the actual work rather than relying only on the floor area or number of rooms. Preparation, access, repairs, furniture protection, drying times and the number of trades can have a significant effect on the total.

Labour estimate

Break the project into activities such as preparation, repairs, priming, painting, flooring and finishing. Estimate the time and skills needed for each activity, then consider how access, room size, surface condition and working restrictions affect productivity.

Materials estimate

List paint, primer, filler, abrasives, masking materials, protective coverings, flooring, underlay, adhesives and other consumables where relevant. Quantities should be based on measurements and manufacturer coverage information, with sensible allowances for waste and surface condition.

Other project costs

Consider delivery, parking, waste removal, access equipment, specialist subcontractors and any agreed protection or cleaning costs. State whether VAT applies and whether materials are included in the quoted price.

Stage 4: Create a realistic project schedule

A project schedule sets out what needs to happen, when it should happen and what must be completed first. It should include trade availability, material lead times, access arrangements, drying and curing periods, inspections and client decisions.

Plan the order of work

Many projects follow a broad sequence: initial protection and removal work, first-fix repairs or alterations, plastering and surface preparation, flooring or other installations as appropriate, decorating, second-fix details, snagging and cleaning. The exact order depends on the scope and the products being used.

Allow for drying and curing

Filler, plaster, primer, paint, adhesives, levelling compounds and flooring finishes all have their own drying or curing requirements. Do not schedule the next activity solely because a calendar slot is available. Follow the product instructions and allow suitable conditions and time.

Confirm trade availability and deliveries

Check that each trade is available when required and that materials will arrive before they are needed. Late deliveries or unavailable subcontractors can affect several activities, so identify critical items early and confirm arrangements in advance.

Build in contingency

Existing properties can contain hidden defects that are not visible during the first survey. Allow realistic flexibility for discoveries, repairs, weather where relevant and client decisions. Explain that contingency is time or budget reserved for uncertainty, not permission to change the scope without approval.

Stage 5: Coordinate trades and responsibilities

When more than one trade is involved, coordination becomes essential. Each person should understand the work area, expected start and finish, access arrangements, site rules and what must be completed before the next activity begins.

Define who is responsible for each task

Record who supplies materials, who protects completed work, who removes waste, who checks measurements and who obtains client approval. If the client appoints separate contractors, make the boundaries between their responsibilities clear.

Manage access and site readiness

Confirm that the work area is available, furniture has been moved or protected as agreed, and preceding trades have completed their tasks. Avoid sending a decorator into a room where wet plaster, unfinished electrical work or unresolved leaks make the area unsuitable.

Keep communication consistent

Use an agreed channel for updates and decisions. Short progress reports can cover completed work, activities planned next, deliveries, issues requiring a decision and any effect on cost or timing. Important instructions and approvals should be recorded in writing.

Stage 6: Monitor progress, quality and costs

Project monitoring compares what is happening on site with the agreed scope, schedule and budget. Regular checks help identify delays, quality issues or unexpected costs while there is still time to respond.

Track progress against the programme

Record completed activities, work in progress and tasks that cannot start because of a dependency. If an activity is delayed, identify the cause and assess which later tasks may be affected. Update the programme and communicate realistic revised dates.

Carry out quality checks at the right stages

Inspect preparation before it is covered, check repairs before decorating, review flooring installation before trims conceal edges, and inspect completed paintwork in suitable lighting. Agree the expected standard with the client before work begins so that quality checks are based on the scope rather than assumptions.

Monitor costs and commitments

Track actual expenditure alongside the estimate, including materials ordered, subcontractor commitments and approved variations. Identify cost pressure early and explain the reason, options and likely impact before making a decision.

Stage 7: Manage variations and unexpected work

A variation is a change to the original scope, specification, cost or programme. It may arise because the client requests a different finish, a hidden defect is discovered or the original assumptions prove incorrect.

Before additional work proceeds, describe what has changed, why it is needed, the additional cost or saving, and the effect on timing. Obtain the appropriate approval in writing and update the project records. For urgent safety or damage issues, follow the agreed escalation process and document the action taken.

Good practice: avoid relying on informal conversations for changes that affect price, materials or completion dates. A short written variation record helps protect both the client and the contractor.

Stage 8: Snagging, final inspection and handover

As the project approaches completion, carry out a structured walkthrough with the client or their representative. Compare the finished work with the agreed scope and record any outstanding items clearly.

Prepare a snagging list

Record each outstanding item, its location, the action required and who is responsible. Distinguish incomplete work from damage, defects or requests that fall outside the agreed scope. Set a realistic date for agreed corrective work.

Provide useful project information

Where available, hand over paint colours and product details, flooring information, care instructions, relevant warranties and records of completed specialist work. Explain any maintenance requirements and allow products to cure for the period specified by their manufacturers.

Close the project clearly

Confirm completion status, outstanding items, agreed final account and any documents still to be supplied. Keep project records, approvals and relevant communications in an organised place.

What should a decorating project plan include?

A practical project plan does not need to be complicated. For a small refurbishment, a clear document and simple schedule may be sufficient. Larger or more complex projects may need a more detailed programme, cost tracker, risk register and formal reporting process.

  • Project details: property, client contact, site address and key dates.
  • Scope: areas, tasks, products, finish expectations, exclusions and assumptions.
  • Budget: labour, materials, subcontractors, logistics, contingency and VAT treatment.
  • Programme: activity sequence, durations, dependencies, milestones and access arrangements.
  • Responsibilities: client, project manager, trades, suppliers and approval owners.
  • Materials: product specifications, quantities, suppliers, lead times and delivery dates.
  • Risks and constraints: access, occupied areas, moisture, hidden defects, safety and approvals.
  • Quality checks: inspection points, agreed standards and snagging process.
  • Change control: how variations are priced, approved and recorded.
  • Handover: completion records, care information, warranties and outstanding actions.

Project management for London properties

Refurbishment work in London can involve tight access, parking restrictions, shared entrances, leasehold requirements, limited storage and neighbours living close to the work area. These practical issues can affect deliveries, waste removal, working hours and the order in which rooms can be completed.

For flats and occupied homes, agree how access will be managed, which areas remain usable, how dust and noise will be controlled and how shared spaces will be protected. Check any building management requirements before arranging deliveries or noisy work. If specialist approvals or regulated work are involved, make sure the correct competent professionals are appointed.

Project management FAQs

What does a decorating project manager do?

A decorating project manager helps define the scope, plan the sequence of work, coordinate trades and materials, monitor progress, communicate changes and check that agreed work is completed. The exact responsibilities should be agreed at the start.

Why is a site survey important before decorating?

A site survey helps establish dimensions, surface conditions, repairs, access restrictions and the required finish. It provides a more reliable basis for the quotation, material estimate and schedule.

How do you schedule painting and other trades?

Plan around dependencies, trade availability, deliveries, access and product drying or curing times. Confirm who is responsible for each task and allow contingency for unexpected work.

How can decorating project costs be controlled?

Define the scope, record assumptions and exclusions, monitor actual and committed costs, and agree variations in writing before additional work proceeds.

What should be included in a decorating project handover?

Include a final walkthrough, completed-work record, outstanding items, product and colour details where available, care guidance and relevant warranties or certificates.

Conclusion: good coordination creates a better project

Painting and decorating projects run more smoothly when the scope is clear, the property is properly surveyed and each stage is planned around the work that comes before it. Realistic budgets, reliable trade coordination, regular quality checks and written approval of changes help keep the project organised and expectations clear.

Whether the work involves repainting a few rooms or coordinating a wider refurbishment, a structured approach helps clients understand what is happening, what decisions are needed and what completion should look like.
