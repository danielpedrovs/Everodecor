Woodwork Painting: A Complete Guide to a Professional Finish

By Daniel Pedro V Sousa
Professional Painter & Decorator at EveroDecor

Doors, skirting boards, architraves and window frames might not take up as much space as your walls, but they have a big influence on how a room looks. Clean lines and a smooth, durable finish can make an interior feel properly finished. The secret is not simply choosing a good paint: preparation, the right primer and careful application all matter.

This guide explains how to approach interior woodwork painting, from assessing the existing finish to preparing the surface, choosing paint and applying the final coats.

Practical note: The correct preparation and coating system depends on the material and condition of the woodwork. Always check the product data sheet, particularly when working over old paint, bare timber or previously varnished surfaces.

1. What Counts as Interior Woodwork?

In decorating, the term woodwork usually refers to the smaller architectural and joinery details around a room. These surfaces are often painted in a contrasting colour or finish to the walls.

  • Skirting boards: the boards running along the bottom of the walls.
  • Architraves: the trim surrounding doors and other openings.
  • Internal doors: panelled, flush, glazed and other styles of door.
  • Window frames and sills: timber surfaces inside the property.
  • Stair parts: handrails, spindles, newel posts and painted stair components.
  • Other joinery: picture rails, dado rails, panelling and built-in features.

Not all these surfaces are necessarily solid timber. Some may be MDF, veneer, previously varnished timber or factory-coated materials. Identifying the substrate is important because it affects the preparation and primer required.

2. Inspect the Existing Finish Before You Start

Before sanding or applying paint, check what is already on the surface. A painted skirting board in good condition needs a different level of preparation from a door with peeling paint or a window sill with water damage.

Check for peeling or flaking paint

Loose coatings need to be removed back to a sound edge. Painting over peeling paint will not make it stable, and the new finish may detach with the old coating.

Look for gloss and contamination

Older woodwork may have a hard, glossy finish. Grease, polish, hand marks and household dirt can also interfere with adhesion. Clean the surface with a suitable cleaner and prepare it according to the coating manufacturer's recommendations.

Check for damage and moisture

Look for dents, open joints, cracked filler, soft timber and signs of water ingress. If timber is damp or decaying, deal with the cause and repair or replace affected material before painting.

3. Preparation: The Most Important Part of Woodwork Painting

A durable finish starts with a clean, sound surface. Rushing preparation is one of the most common reasons that painted woodwork looks rough or begins to fail.

  1. Protect the room: cover floors and nearby furniture. Use suitable masking where it will help protect adjacent surfaces.
  2. Clean the woodwork: remove dust, grease, polish and other contamination with a suitable cleaning product.
  3. Remove loose paint: scrape back flaking or poorly adhered coatings carefully, taking appropriate precautions around old paint.
  4. Sand the surface: lightly abrade sound painted finishes to reduce gloss and create a key. Use a suitable grade of abrasive for the material and condition.
  5. Feather damaged edges: smooth the transition between existing paint and exposed areas so that repairs do not leave obvious ridges.
  6. Remove dust: vacuum and wipe down using a method suitable for the surface, then allow it to dry.
  7. Complete repairs: fill suitable defects and allow fillers to dry before sanding them smooth.

Protecting the surrounding area and preparing surfaces carefully helps keep the job controlled. Image: Unsplash.

If the existing coating is very old or its composition is unknown, do not dry-sand or use heat stripping without first considering the risk. Older paint may contain lead. In that situation, use appropriate testing and follow current UK safety guidance or use a competent professional.

4. Filling Gaps, Dents and Cracks

Small imperfections can stand out once the woodwork has been painted, particularly when light falls across a glossy or satin finish. Filling and sanding should therefore be completed before the finishing coats.

  • Nail holes and small dents: use a suitable wood filler or repair product.
  • Small gaps around trim: a paintable decorator's caulk can be suitable for appropriate joints between trim and walls.
  • Open joints in timber: assess whether the joint needs a more suitable repair rather than simply filling the visible gap.
  • Rot or soft timber: establish the cause and repair or replace damaged material. Ordinary filler is not a substitute for sound timber.

Allow fillers and caulk to dry as directed. Sand repairs smooth where appropriate and remove all dust before priming or painting. Avoid using flexible caulk in places where a hard wood filler or a different repair system is required.

5. Choosing the Right Primer

Primer helps create a suitable surface for the next coating, but there is no universal primer for every type of woodwork. Choose it according to the substrate, existing finish and paint system.

Bare timber

Bare wood normally needs a suitable wood primer before the finishing coats. Some timber contains knots or resin that can discolour paint, so the correct knotting or stain-blocking treatment may also be required. Follow the product instructions and check whether the primer is suitable for the timber species and intended topcoat.

MDF

MDF edges can be more absorbent than the face and may need particular attention. Use a primer designed for MDF or the specified substrate, and make sure cut edges are properly sealed before applying the finishing coats.

Previously painted woodwork

If the old coating is sound, clean and compatible with the new paint, a full primer coat may not always be necessary. Bare patches, repairs, stains or difficult surfaces may need spot priming or a suitable adhesion primer.

Varnished or glossy surfaces

These surfaces need careful cleaning and preparation. Depending on the product system, thorough abrasion and a specialist adhesion primer may be required. Do not assume ordinary wall primer will bond properly to varnish or a hard gloss coating.

Good practice: Check the technical data sheet for the primer and topcoat. Confirm that the products are compatible, note the recommended preparation and observe the stated recoat times.

6. Choosing Paint for Interior Woodwork

Woodwork paint is designed for surfaces that are handled, touched and cleaned more frequently than walls. Doors, skirting boards and handrails can receive knocks and everyday wear, so the product should be suitable for the level of use.

Water-based trim paint

Modern water-based trim paints are popular for interior woodwork. They generally have lower odour than traditional solvent-based products and often dry more quickly. Many are formulated to provide a durable finish on correctly prepared doors, frames and skirting boards.

Oil-based or solvent-based paint

Traditional oil-based products can provide a hard-wearing finish, but they typically have a stronger odour and longer drying times. Some formulations may yellow over time, particularly in areas with limited natural light. Compatibility with the existing coating is important when changing systems.

Specialist products

Some surfaces need specialist coatings, such as products for floors, radiators, kitchens or high-wear joinery. Use a paint specifically approved for the surface and conditions rather than choosing a product on appearance alone.

When comparing products, look at durability, application, drying time, recoat time, recommended primer and cleaning instructions. The best choice is the one that suits the surface and the way it will be used.

7. Understanding Woodwork Paint Finishes

The sheen level changes how woodwork looks and how it relates to the walls. The names used by manufacturers can vary, so check the actual finish and sample it in the room before committing.

  • Matt: a low-sheen appearance that can create a contemporary, understated look. Check the product's durability and suitability for trim.
  • Eggshell: a subtle sheen that is often used on interior woodwork and can give a softer appearance than gloss.
  • Satin: a gentle sheen that reflects some light and is a popular choice for doors, skirting boards and frames.
  • Semi-gloss: a more reflective finish that can highlight profiles and details.
  • Gloss: a high-sheen finish that creates a traditional, reflective appearance but can make surface imperfections more noticeable.

A higher sheen does not automatically mean a better finish. Gloss and semi-gloss can emphasise dents, brush marks and uneven preparation. If the woodwork is old or heavily marked, extra preparation may be needed before choosing a reflective finish.

8. How to Paint Woodwork for a Smooth Finish

Once preparation and priming are complete, careful application helps produce a consistent result. Always follow the paint manufacturer's instructions for application, thinning, temperature and recoat times.

Doors

Start by identifying the door type and the best way to work across its sections. On a panelled door, paint the recessed panels and mouldings carefully before moving to the rails and stiles. On a flush door, use a consistent method and maintain a wet edge to reduce lap marks. Avoid overworking paint as it begins to set.

Skirting boards and architraves

Use a suitable brush for edges and profiles. Work in manageable lengths, keeping the application even and checking for runs along the bottom edges and inside corners. Protect the floor and adjacent wall finish.

Window frames and sills

Check the condition of joints and exposed timber, particularly where condensation or water may have affected the surface. Make sure moving parts, drainage paths and hardware are not painted over. Use a coating suitable for the location and exposure.

Stair rails and spindles

These parts are handled frequently, so durability and correct curing are important. Work systematically around the profiles and allow sufficient time before putting the surfaces back into heavy use.

  1. Stir the paint thoroughly, following the manufacturer's instructions.
  2. Use a good-quality brush or mini roller suited to the product and profile.
  3. Apply an even coat without overloading the brush.
  4. Watch for drips, especially underneath rails, mouldings and horizontal edges.
  5. Allow the coat to dry for the stated time before lightly preparing it if the product system requires it.
  6. Apply the next coat evenly and allow the finish to cure before heavy use.

Drying and curing are not the same thing. A surface may feel touch-dry well before it reaches its full durability. Avoid repeated handling, cleaning or knocking freshly painted woodwork until the product has cured as recommended.

9. Common Woodwork Painting Mistakes

Painting over dirt or grease

Contamination can prevent paint from bonding properly. Clean the surface first and allow it to dry before sanding or coating.

Skipping the sanding stage

A glossy surface may not provide a suitable key for the next coat. Prepare sound paint according to the manufacturer's instructions rather than applying new paint straight over a shiny finish.

Using the wrong primer

A primer designed for bare timber may not be suitable for glossy laminate, varnish or a stained surface. Match the primer to the substrate and topcoat.

Applying paint too thickly

Heavy coats can sag, run or take longer to dry. Several correctly applied coats usually give a more controlled result than trying to achieve full coverage in one thick application.

Rushing between coats

Applying another coat before the previous one is ready can affect the finish. Observe the stated recoat time and take account of the room conditions.

Ignoring the edges and details

Runs underneath skirting boards, paint on hinges and rough edges around door panels can spoil an otherwise good job. Inspect the work from different angles as you go.

10. Should You Paint Woodwork Before or After the Walls?

The best sequence depends on the condition of the room and the work involved. A common approach is to complete the messy preparation first, then work through the main surfaces before finishing the trim.

  1. Complete repairs, sanding and preparation on the walls and woodwork.
  2. Prime bare or repaired woodwork and prepare any other surfaces that need priming.
  3. Paint the ceiling, followed by the walls.
  4. Apply the finishing coats to doors, skirting boards, architraves and other trim.
  5. Complete careful touch-ups and remove masking at the appropriate stage.

Some decorators prefer to apply an initial coat to the woodwork earlier in the process, then complete the final coat after the walls. The important point is to plan the sequence so that fresh finishes are protected and any cutting-in can be completed neatly.

11. What Affects the Cost of Woodwork Painting?

The cost depends on the amount of woodwork, its condition and the preparation required. A room with a few sound skirting boards is very different from a property with multiple panelled doors, detailed architraves and old coatings that need extensive preparation.

  • Number and size of items: doors, frames, skirting boards, windows, stair parts and built-in joinery.
  • Profiles and detail: plain flush doors are generally quicker to prepare and paint than heavily moulded or panelled surfaces.
  • Existing condition: peeling paint, dents, damaged timber and failed joints require additional work.
  • Preparation level: cleaning, scraping, sanding, filling, caulking and priming.
  • Colour and coating change: a significant colour change or change of paint system may require additional coats or specialist primer.
  • Access and protection: occupied rooms, furniture, staircases and difficult access can affect the time required.

For a useful quotation, provide photographs and a list of the items to be painted. Make clear whether the price includes repairs, materials, priming and the number of finishing coats.

12. Looking After Newly Painted Woodwork

Once the paint has cured, regular gentle cleaning can help keep the finish looking fresh. Use a soft cloth and a mild cleaning method suitable for the paint. Avoid abrasive pads and strong chemicals unless the manufacturer confirms they are safe for the coating.

Deal with chips and knocks before moisture gets underneath the paint film. In areas exposed to condensation or water, check that ventilation is adequate and that the underlying cause of any moisture is resolved.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the best paint for interior woodwork?

It depends on the surface, level of use and desired finish. Quality water-based trim paints are popular for doors, skirting boards and frames because they generally have lower odour and faster drying. Oil-based products may also be suitable in some situations, provided the existing coating and preparation are compatible.

Do I need to sand woodwork before painting?

Usually, yes. Sound painted woodwork should normally be cleaned and lightly sanded to reduce gloss and provide a suitable key. Bare, damaged or peeling areas need more thorough preparation.

Do I need primer on previously painted woodwork?

Not always. If the existing paint is sound and the new coating is compatible, suitable preparation may be enough. Bare patches, repairs, stains and difficult surfaces normally need the appropriate primer.

How many coats of paint does woodwork need?

It depends on the product, colour change and condition of the surface. A typical system may include primer where needed followed by two finishing coats, but always follow the paint manufacturer's instructions.

Should I paint woodwork before or after the walls?

A common sequence is to prepare and prime the woodwork, paint the ceiling and walls, then apply the final woodwork coats and complete touch-ups. The best order can change depending on the room and the work involved.

Can I paint over varnished wood?

It may be possible, but the surface needs thorough cleaning and suitable preparation. Depending on the coating and the paint system, abrasion and a specialist adhesion primer may be required. Check product compatibility before starting.
