Why I Always Reach for Mesh on Cracked Walls

Filler alone will not hold a crack that moves. Here is the method I use on every job where the crack has come back before.

By Daniel, painter and decorator, EveroDecor 16 September 2026 6 minute read

I get asked about this a lot, so I wanted to write down how I actually think about it, rather than just the short answer I give on site.

If you have ever filled a crack in a wall and watched it come straight back a few months later, you already know the problem. Filler on its own is stiff. Walls, especially older plaster or ones near a door frame or a stair void, move a tiny bit with the seasons. That movement finds the weakest point, which is almost always your freshly filled crack, and it opens up again. It is not that the filler was bad. It is that filler was never designed to bridge movement on its own.

That is where mesh comes in. I mean the self adhesive fibreglass mesh tape, the kind that looks like a loose weave bandage. You lay it directly over the crack before you fill, and it acts like a little skeleton under the surface. The filler still does the job of making things flat and smooth, but now it has something to grip onto and spread the strain across, instead of concentrating it all along one thin line.

In short

  • Filler is rigid, walls move, so the repair fails along its weakest line.
  • Mesh tape spreads that movement instead of letting it focus on one crack.
  • Feathering the filler wide is what makes the repair disappear under paint.

When I use it

I do not reach for mesh on every hairline crack. If it is a tiny settlement crack, the sort you see on a new build in its first year or two, I will usually just fill and move on, because a bit of flexible filler handles that fine. Mesh earns its place on the cracks that keep coming back, the ones that run along a join between plasterboard sheets, or across a ceiling near a loft hatch, or on stairwells where the whole structure gets a bit of daily flex. Basically, anywhere I suspect the wall is going to keep moving after I have left.

In the older housing stock around Notting Hill and Chelsea, I use it more often than not. Lath and plaster ceilings and heavily patched Victorian walls move more than people expect.

How I actually apply it

What I use

For most domestic crack repairs I reach for a self adhesive fibreglass mesh tape, 50mm wide. The No Nonsense Fibreglass Mesh Tape (90m x 50mm) from Screwfix is the one I keep in the van. It is corrosion resistant, odourless, and a 90 metre roll goes a long way across a single job. Any 50mm self adhesive fibreglass mesh will do the same job; the width and the self adhesive backing are what matter, not the brand.

Product photo: Screwfix

  1. Open up and clean the crack

    Rake out the crack a little so there is something for the filler to key into, and brush away the dust.

  2. Bed the tape over the crack

    Peel the backing off the mesh tape and press it straight over the crack, centred, with a bit of overlap either side.

  3. Skim the first coat

    Skim a thin coat of filler or joint compound over the top, feathering it out wide so there is no ridge.

  4. Dry, then sand flush

    Let it dry fully, then sand it back until it is flush with the wall.

  5. Second thin coat

    A second, thinner coat afterwards makes the finish disappear completely once it is painted.

The key thing people miss is the feathering. If you just blob filler straight over the tape in a thick strip, you will see it under the paint, especially in raking light from a window. Wide and thin beats narrow and thick every time.

A couple of things I have learned the hard way

Do not skip the mesh and go straight for a heavier filler thinking that will do the same job. It will not. A stiff, thick filler without reinforcement just cracks along a new line right next to the old one.

Also, paint is not a fix. I have been called out to houses where someone painted straight over a moving crack more than once, and it just telegraphs straight back through. Mesh first, then filler, then paint, in that order, every time.

If you are dealing with a crack that keeps returning and you are not sure whether it is a movement issue or something structural worth getting checked, that is a different conversation, and always worth asking about rather than guessing.

Common questions about wall cracks

Why does a filled wall crack keep coming back?

Because filler is rigid and the wall is not. Seasonal movement concentrates along the line of the repair, which is the weakest point, and the crack reopens. Reinforcing with mesh spreads that strain rather than letting it focus on one line.

Do I need mesh tape on every hairline crack?

No. Minor settlement cracks in a new build usually only need a flexible filler. Mesh is worth using on cracks that have returned before, on plasterboard joins, on ceilings near a loft hatch, and in stairwells where the structure flexes.

Can I just paint over a crack in the wall?

Paint will not hold a moving crack closed. It comes back through the finish, often within a season. The order that works is mesh, then filler, then paint.

What is the difference between mesh tape and paper tape?

Fibreglass mesh tape is self adhesive and lets filler pass through the weave, which suits crack repair and patching. Paper tape is bedded into compound and gives a slightly flatter result on new plasterboard joints, but it is less forgiving to apply.

When is a wall crack a structural problem?

Cracks wider than roughly five millimetres, cracks running diagonally from window or door corners, and cracks that keep widening are worth having assessed by a surveyor before any decorating starts.
