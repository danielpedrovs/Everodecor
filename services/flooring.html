Flooring Guide: Engineered Wood, Laminate, Underlay & Professional Installation

Choosing a new floor involves more than selecting a colour or wood pattern. The subfloor, moisture conditions, underlay, installation method and room layout all affect the finished result. This guide explains the main options and the preparation behind a well-installed floor.

Published: 7 November 2026
Published by: EveroDecor – Professional Painting, Decorating & Flooring, London
Editor & Adaptation: Daniel Pedro V Sousa

A new floor can change the feel of an entire property. Natural timber brings warmth and character, engineered wood offers a real wood surface with a layered construction, and laminate provides a wide choice of decorative finishes. Each option has different installation requirements, maintenance considerations and costs.

One of the most important parts of any flooring project is often hidden once the work is complete: the subfloor. If the base is damp, unstable, dusty or uneven, even an expensive floor can develop movement, noise or joint problems. Before ordering materials, inspect the existing floor and confirm that the proposed product is suitable for the room and installation conditions.

The short answer: choose flooring to suit the room, inspect and test the subfloor, correct moisture and level issues, then select the installation system specified by the manufacturer. Underlay is commonly used with floating floors, while fully bonded engineered wood is installed with a compatible adhesive directly onto a properly prepared subfloor. Allow for expansion and movement wherever the product instructions require it.

Which type of flooring should you choose?

The main options for residential projects include laminate, engineered wood, solid wood and parquet. The right choice depends on the appearance you want, the condition of the property, the level of foot traffic and the maintenance you are prepared to carry out.

Laminate flooring

Laminate flooring is made from a composite core with a printed decorative layer and a protective wear surface. It is available in many wood-effect designs and is often selected for its practical maintenance and range of price points. Most laminate floors are installed as floating click systems over a suitable underlay, although product designs vary.

Laminate is not the same as real timber and damaged boards are not generally sanded and refinished like a solid wood floor. Pay attention to the product's water-resistance claims and the manufacturer's restrictions for kitchens, bathrooms and other areas exposed to moisture.

Engineered wood flooring

Engineered wood has a real timber wear layer bonded to a layered core. This construction can offer greater dimensional stability than a comparable solid wood board, but it remains a natural wood product and responds to changes in temperature and humidity.

It is available in different thicknesses, surface finishes and formats. Depending on the product, it may be installed as a floating floor, fully bonded with adhesive or mechanically fixed. The wear-layer thickness affects how the floor can be maintained or refinished, so check the manufacturer's specifications before buying.

Solid wood flooring

Solid wood boards are made from a single piece of timber. They can be sanded and refinished where sufficient material remains, but they are more sensitive to changes in moisture and environmental conditions than many engineered products. Installation method, subfloor type and room conditions are particularly important.

Parquet, herringbone and chevron flooring

Parquet describes flooring made from smaller pieces arranged in a pattern. Herringbone uses rectangular blocks laid at right angles, while chevron boards are cut or manufactured with angled ends to create a continuous V pattern. These layouts can create a distinctive finish but require accurate setting-out, consistent alignment and careful preparation.

Subfloor preparation: the foundation of a good floor

The subfloor is the surface that supports the new flooring. It may be concrete, screed, plywood, chipboard or existing timber floorboards. Each type needs to be checked before installation. A new floor should not be expected to hide significant dips, movement, damp or structural defects.

Check that the subfloor is sound

Look for loose boards, squeaks, movement, cracks, crumbling screed, old adhesive and damaged sections. Timber boards may need to be secured, while weak or broken areas may require repair or replacement. Concrete and screed should be sound and suitable for the selected adhesive or floating system.

Check flatness and level

Flooring manufacturers specify how flat the base must be, and tolerances differ between products. Use a suitable straightedge or long level to identify high spots and dips. Localised defects may need sanding, filling or a compatible levelling compound. Do not assume that a thicker underlay will correct an uneven subfloor.

Check moisture before installation

Moisture is a major risk for wood-based flooring. Concrete and screed may retain construction moisture, while timber floors can be affected by leaks, poor ventilation or damp from below. Use an appropriate moisture-testing method for the substrate and compare the results with the flooring and adhesive manufacturers' limits.

If readings are outside the permitted range, investigate the cause and follow the specified moisture-control system. A surface coating or membrane should not be selected without checking compatibility with the substrate, adhesive and flooring product.

Clean the surface thoroughly

Remove dust, grit, loose material and contamination. Adhesive systems may require a specific primer and surface preparation. A floating system still needs a clean, stable base so that debris does not create noise or damage the underside of the flooring.

Underlay: what does it do and when do you need it?

Underlay is a layer installed between the subfloor and certain floor coverings. Depending on its design, it can help with acoustic performance, thermal insulation, minor surface texture and moisture management. It must be compatible with the flooring system and the subfloor.

Underlay for laminate flooring

Most laminate products require an underlay unless an integrated backing is specified. Underlay type and thickness should match the manufacturer's requirements. Some products require a separate damp-proof membrane over mineral subfloors, while others use an underlay with an integrated moisture barrier. Joints and overlaps must be treated as specified.

Underlay for floating engineered wood

Floating engineered wood floors commonly use a suitable acoustic or combination underlay. The product must be designed for the floor's locking or joint system and the subfloor beneath it. An underlay that is too soft or too thick can allow excessive movement and put stress on the joints.

Does glue-down engineered wood need underlay?

A fully bonded engineered wood floor is normally fixed directly to a suitable prepared subfloor using the specified flooring adhesive. It is not installed as a floating floor over a compressible underlay. The adhesive, primer and any moisture-control products must form a compatible system.

Important: underlay is not a universal extra layer. A floating floor and a fully bonded floor are different installation systems. Confirm the installation method before buying underlay, adhesive or moisture-control products.

Floating vs glue-down flooring: what is the difference?

The installation method affects the feel of the floor, the preparation required and the materials used. The flooring manufacturer may permit one method or several methods for a particular product.

Floating installation

With a floating floor, the boards are joined to one another rather than fixed directly to the subfloor. Many laminate and click-fit engineered floors use this method. A suitable underlay is normally installed beneath the floor unless the product has an integrated backing and its instructions state otherwise.

Floating floors need the specified expansion gaps around walls, door frames, pipes and other fixed objects. Large continuous areas or connected rooms may need additional movement joints or transition profiles.

Glue-down installation

With a glue-down system, the flooring is bonded directly to a suitable subfloor using a compatible flooring adhesive. This method is commonly used for certain engineered wood and parquet products. It requires careful subfloor preparation, moisture checks, correct adhesive selection and the specified trowel and application method.

Which method is best?

There is no single best method for every project. The choice depends on the flooring product, subfloor construction, room conditions, acoustic requirements and manufacturer's instructions. Do not choose a method based only on whether it appears quicker or uses fewer materials.

Expansion gaps and movement joints

Wood and laminate flooring can expand and contract as temperature and humidity change. Floating floors therefore need space to move at the perimeter and around fixed objects. The required gap depends on the product, room dimensions and installation instructions.

Skirting boards, scotia or suitable trim can cover perimeter gaps, but they should not trap the floor or prevent movement. Doorways, narrow passages, large connected areas, pipes and columns may need additional detailing or transition profiles. Follow the flooring manufacturer's layout requirements.

How to install engineered wood flooring

Once the subfloor has passed the required checks and the installation method has been confirmed, the work can be planned. The exact sequence varies between floating, nailed and glue-down products.

  1. Check the site conditions: confirm that wet trades are complete, the building is weather-tight and room conditions meet the product requirements.
  2. Inspect the delivery: check product, grade, dimensions, finish and quantity before installation. Report damage or discrepancies to the supplier.
  3. Follow acclimatisation instructions: store the flooring in the conditions and for the period specified by the manufacturer. Not all products have the same requirements.
  4. Prepare the subfloor: complete moisture, flatness and stability checks, and carry out the required repairs, priming or moisture treatment.
  5. Plan the layout: measure the room, consider the direction of the boards, set out starting lines and avoid very narrow finishing strips where possible.
  6. Blend boards where appropriate: mix boards from several packs as recommended to distribute natural colour and grain variation.
  7. Install using the specified system: fit the underlay for a floating floor or apply the approved adhesive for a glue-down system.
  8. Maintain movement allowances: use spacers and form expansion gaps and transition details as required.
  9. Complete edges and thresholds: fit suitable profiles and trims without restricting floor movement.
  10. Protect the finished floor: follow the manufacturer's guidance for cleaning, curing, furniture placement and protection during other building work.

How to lay laminate flooring

Laminate is commonly installed as a floating click system. The subfloor should be clean, dry, stable and within the manufacturer's flatness tolerance. Use the correct underlay and moisture barrier for the substrate, unless the product specifies an integrated solution.

  1. Remove existing floor coverings where required and inspect the base.
  2. Secure loose timber boards and repair uneven or damaged areas.
  3. Check moisture and install any specified damp-proof membrane.
  4. Lay compatible underlay with joints treated as instructed.
  5. Plan the board direction and stagger end joints according to the product guidance.
  6. Fit boards using the manufacturer's locking method and suitable tools.
  7. Maintain perimeter gaps and install transition profiles where required.
  8. Fit skirting or trims and clean the surface using the recommended method.

Some laminate products have integrated underlay or special requirements for underfloor heating. Always use the instructions supplied with the exact product rather than assuming that every click floor is installed in the same way.

Can an existing timber floor be sanded and refinished?

In some cases, an existing timber floor can be restored rather than replaced. Sanding removes the worn surface layer and prepares the timber for a new finish, such as lacquer, oil or hardwax oil. Whether this is possible depends on the floor construction, remaining wear layer, previous treatments and condition of the boards.

Inspect the floor before sanding

Check for loose boards, movement, deep stains, water damage, previous repairs and protruding nails or fixings. Engineered flooring can only be sanded within the limits of its real timber wear layer. Some veneer products are not designed for sanding at all.

Choose the right finish

Lacquer, oil and hardwax oil create different appearances and maintenance requirements. The choice should suit the timber, the existing coating and the expected use of the room. Follow the finish manufacturer's preparation, application and curing instructions.

How much flooring should you order?

Measure the length and width of each room and calculate its area. Add the room areas together, then allow an additional quantity for cutting, board selection and the flooring pattern. The amount of extra material depends on the product, room shape, installation layout and supplier guidance.

For a straightforward rectangular room, area is calculated by multiplying length by width. For example, a room measuring 5 metres by 4 metres has a floor area of 20 square metres. Hallways, alcoves, bay areas and irregular rooms should be measured separately and added to the total.

Herringbone and chevron layouts can require more cutting and careful selection than a straight plank layout. Confirm the recommended waste allowance with the flooring supplier before ordering. It is also sensible to retain spare boards from the same batch for future repairs, where possible.

What affects the cost of a flooring project?

The total cost is influenced by more than the price per square metre of the flooring. A reliable estimate should distinguish materials, preparation and installation labour so that the scope is clear.

Planning flooring work in London homes

London properties can have a wide range of subfloor constructions, from suspended timber floors in period homes to concrete or screeded bases in newer flats. Older properties may also have uneven floorboards, previous repairs, different floor heights between rooms or restricted access for material deliveries.

For larger projects, plan the work room by room and check how the new floor will meet existing tiles, carpet, stairs and thresholds. In flats, consider building management rules, delivery access, noise restrictions and the need to protect shared areas. Confirm any underfloor heating requirements before choosing the flooring system.

Flooring FAQs

Is engineered wood flooring better than laminate?

Neither is automatically better. Engineered wood has a real timber wear layer, while laminate has a decorative surface over a composite core. Compare appearance, cost, maintenance, moisture restrictions and the product's installation requirements.

Does engineered wood flooring need underlay?

It depends on the installation method. Floating engineered floors commonly use a suitable underlay. Fully bonded glue-down floors are fixed directly to the prepared subfloor with the specified adhesive and do not normally use floating-floor underlay.

Does laminate flooring need underlay?

Most laminate systems require a compatible underlay unless an integrated backing is specified. The underlay should match the product and subfloor, and it should not be used to conceal significant unevenness.

Does a subfloor need to be level?

Yes. It must meet the flooring manufacturer's requirements for flatness, stability and cleanliness. High spots, dips, loose boards and defects should be addressed before installation.

Do wood floors need expansion gaps?

Most floating wood and laminate floors require perimeter expansion gaps and sometimes additional movement joints. The required dimensions and locations depend on the product and room layout.

Can engineered wood flooring be glued down?

Many engineered wood products can be fully bonded to a suitable prepared subfloor using the specified flexible flooring adhesive. Check the product and adhesive instructions before choosing this method.

Conclusion: the best flooring starts underneath

The appearance of a new floor matters, but its long-term performance depends on the preparation beneath it. Choose a product that suits the room, check the subfloor carefully, manage moisture, select the correct underlay or adhesive and allow for natural movement. A well-planned installation creates a cleaner finish and helps reduce problems after the room is back in use.
